159	Chapter 1:Introduction
160	Sovereign debt refers to a national government’s total financial obligations to domestic and foreign creditors, usually through bonds and loans. Sovereign debt defaults occur when countries fail to meet these debt obligations, triggering wide ranging consequences, including currency devaluation, investor panic, and regional contagion (Reinhart & Rogoff, 2009). As a result, sovereign debt poses a serious threat to global financial stability and national welfare (Mallucci, 2021). Early warning systems for sovereign default risk are critical for policymakers, investors, and international financial institutions (IMF, 2023). However, reliable prediction remains difficult due to the fact that complex interplay of macroeconomic, financial, and political factors influencing sovereign risk (Savona & Vezzoli, 2013). Traditionally, economists have relied on statistical techniques such as probit and logit models or indicator based thresholds to forecast debt crises, but these models often have insufficient predictive power and fail to capture nonlinear patterns (Manasse et al., 2003).
161	While many machine learning applications exist in credit risk, few combine XGBoost, LSTM networks, and SHAP explainability specifically for sovereign default prediction. This thesis addresses this research gap by proposing a hybrid machine learning framework that integrates ensemble methods and deep learning to improve prediction accuracy while offering interpretable insights into the drivers of sovereign default. The proposed model is empirically validated using macroeconomic and political data from a diverse set of emerging and developed countries between 1980 and 2022, ensuring robustness and cross contextual generalizability.
162	1.1 Background
163	Historically, sovereign debt crises have affected both emerging and advanced economies. Notable examples include the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s, Russia in 1998, Argentina in 2001 and 2014, and Greece during the 2010 Eurozone crisis (Tomz & Wright, 2007; Savona & Vezzoli, 2013). These crises exposed limitations in traditional early warning models, which missed key signals due to linear assumptions and rigid thresholds. Econometric techniques such as logistic regression and probit models demonstrate limited flexibility to capture complex, nonlinear interactions between economic and political variables, resulting in poor predictive performance and frequent false positives (Manasse et al., 2003; Fuertes & Kalotychou, 2007).
164	Recent research demonstrates that machine learning (ML) models offer considerable promise for forecasting financial risk, including sovereign defaults. Ensemble methods like XGBoost and Random Forests, and deep learning models such as long short term memory (LSTM) networks, can identify intricate nonlinear relationships in large, high dimensional datasets (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Siami-Namin & Siami-Namin, 2018). These methods have shown strong predictive power in areas such as credit risk and macroeconomic volatility (Hellwig, 2021; Alaminos et al., 2021). Moreover, a major drawback of these advanced models is their perceived black box nature. This absence of interpretability limits practical adoption in policy environments where transparency and trust are essential (Bussmann et al., 2020).
165	Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) tools such as SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) have been developed to address this challenge by quantifying the contribution of each input variable to model predictions (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). SHAP has enhanced transparency in financial applications, improving trust and supporting policy alignment (Hacibedel & Qu, 2022; Ariza-Garzón et al., 2020). Despite the development, such tools remain underutilised in sovereign default prediction.
166	This research proposes a hybrid modelling framework integrating ensemble learning via XGBoost, sequence modelling through attention based LSTM networks, and SHAP interpretability. This approach captures both the cross sectional heterogeneity of macro financial indicators and their temporal evolution while offering interpretable risk signals. The model is tested on annual macroeconomic and political data from  68 countries between 1980 and 2022, providing a robust basis for early warning systems and data driven policy responses.

190	2.1 Scope
191	This review critically examines literature that relates to sovereign debt default prediction, focusing on traditional econometric approaches, machine learning (ML) advancements, and explainability frameworks. The scope includes historical economic crises, methodological developments in predictive analytics, and interpretability practices within financial forecasting.
192	To enhance clarity and maintain focus, broader macroeconomic theories such as general equilibrium models, Keynesian fiscal multipliers, or global growth cycle theories were excluded from this review. Including them would risk diluting the predictive focus of this study and shifting attention away from the variables, models, and techniques specifically used to forecast default risk. Therefore, only theories with direct relevance to sovereign credit events and early warning systems were retained.
193	2.2 Theoretical Background
194	Sovereign default theories stem from foundational economic concepts including debt intolerance, debt sustainability analysis, and the distinction between liquidity and solvency crisis. The strategic default model by Eaton and Gersovitz (1981) introduced the idea that countries may choose to default based on a cost benefit analysis, highlighting the role of strategic behaviour under credit constraints. While influential, such models offer limited predictive application due to their theoretical abstraction and constrained empirical flexibility. More recent theoretical contributions emphasise the importance of nonlinear dynamics and threshold effects in sovereign default processes. For example, small changes in external interest rates or fiscal balance can lead to disproportionately large shifts in default probability once critical levels are crossed, a feature that traditional linear econometric models often fail to detect (Reinhart, Rogoff, & Savastano, 2003; Detragiache & Spilimbergo, 2001).
195	These nonlinearities and tipping points directly inform the design of this study's hybrid modelling approach. Ensemble methods such as XGBoost are well suited to capture complex, non additive interactions between economic indicators, while recurrent neural networks like LSTM are capable of identifying temporal thresholds and patterns in country level time series. By integrating these models, the hybrid framework aligns with modern theoretical understanding that default risk is not simply additive or linear, but evolves through interaction and feedback. This alignment ensures that the empirical strategy is responsive to the conceptual realities underpinning sovereign financial distress.
199	2.3 Case Studies
200	Despite their widespread use, linear econometric models often failed to anticipate key tipping points in major sovereign crises. For example, during Argentina’s 2001 crisis, implied default probabilities derived from bond prices increased sharply from approximately 19.4 per cent to 45.5per cent between October and December 2001, indicating that market recognition of the crisis occurred only after substantial economic deterioration had already begun (Mendoza & Ostry, 2008). In the case of Greece in 2010, estimated default probabilities rose from under 5 percent to around 40 per cent within weeks before falling back below 10 percent following the announcement of the EU and IMF intervention (Savona & Vezzoli, 2015). These abrupt and nonlinear shifts highlight the limitations of traditional regression based approaches, which struggle to capture sudden, context dependent accelerations in sovereign risk. Such empirical breakdowns underscore the need for more flexible modelling strategies, such as hybrid machine learning architectures that combine XGBoost for nonlinear pattern detection with LSTM networks for learning risk evolution over time.While traditional econometric models such as logit, probit, and Cox regression provide interpretability, they rely a lot on assumptions of linearity and independence that rarely hold in sovereign debt dynamics. These simplifications risk underestimating contagion effects, feedback loops, and structural breaks, limiting their policy relevance in volatile contexts.
202	2.4 Traditional Econometric Approaches
203	Traditionally, logistic regression, probit models, and discriminant analysis have dominated sovereign default prediction. Manasse and Roubini (2009), among others within the IMF, have extensively used these methods and found moderate predictive capabilities. Their main strengths lie in interpretability and simplicity; however, these models struggle with nonlinearity and complex interactions. Such limitations often result in poor out of sample performance and excessive false positives.
204	A concrete example is found in the Greek sovereign debt crisis, econometric models relying on logistic or probit specifications failed to adjust to shifting risk environments. During 2010, out of sample default probabilities remained under 10 per cent, but real events drove bond default spreads well above 40 per cent, a divergence that traditional models did not capture (Savona & Vezzoli, 2013). Comparatively, studies confirmed that tree based methods such as random forests correctly identified approximately 75 per cent of fiscal stress or default events, outperforming logit models whose early warning accuracy hovered around 65 per cent (Jarmulska, 2020). This evidence underscores the inadequacy of linear approaches in capturing sudden shifts in sovereign risk, justifying the shift toward more flexible machine learning methods that can model nonlinear thresholds and complex feature interactions.
206	Traditional econometric models underperform in sovereign debt prediction primarily due to their restrictive assumptions. First, they rely on rigid functional forms such as the logit or probit link functions that assume linear relationships between predictors and default probability. This rigidity fails to capture real world nonlinear threshold effects and interacting risk triggers such as simultaneous spikes in debt and political instability (Jarmulska, 2022). Second, the assumption of independence across observations is violated in panel data, where macroeconomic shocks are serially correlated and exhibit heteroskedasticity, leading to biased parameter estimates. Third, as models include more variables, particularly macroeconomic and political ones, they become susceptible to multicollinearity and overfitting, reducing forecasting accuracy. For instance, in fiscal stress prediction, random forest models which can handle correlated predictors achieved 78–80% accuracy compared to 70–75% for probit specifications, revealing traditional models’ inability to manage high dimensional data (Jarmulska, 2022). Finally, econometric models are inherently static, unable to account for temporal dynamics such as debt accumulation or successive fiscal shocks. This omission leads to missed early warning signals when multiple years of adverse changes build up. While these models remain transparent and easily interpretable, their limitations in capturing complexity, dynamics, and nonlinearity justify the adoption of models such as hybrid XGBoost/LSTM architectures that address these very weaknesses.Econometric models such as logit, probit, and hazard rely on assumptions of linearity and error independence, which are seldom met in sovereign datasets. Defaults are rare events, and most studies understate how class imbalance distorts estimation, leading to inflated accuracy but poor recall.Although machine learning approaches improve predictive accuracy, their performance is often highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning and dataset composition. A lack of standardized benchmarks across studies also raises concerns about reproducibility and comparability of results in sovereign default prediction.
209	2.5 Machine Learning Approaches to Sovereign Debt Prediction
210	Advances in machine learning, particularly ensemble methods like Random Forests, XGBoost, and neural networks such as RNNs, have led to significant gains in sovereign default forecasting accuracy. For instance, Hellwig (2021) found that by employing ML techniques such as Elastic Net, Random Forests, and Gradient Boosted Trees, they improved out of sample crisis prediction accuracy by approximately 10 percentage points compared to traditional econometric models. Similarly, a comparative study on sovereign risk modelling demonstrated that XGBoost achieved superior out of sample and out of time performance, increasing classification accuracy from around 85% to 94% over logistic regression benchmarks. Despite these clear performance improvements, these methods are often critiqued as black boxes, which limits their adoption in policymaking contexts that require transparent and interpretable models.
212	Empirical comparisons in sovereign debt and related credit risk models reveal that not all machine learning methods perform equally. A recent study on sovereign debt ratings in South Africa compared 14 ML algorithms and found that XGBoost, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosted Machines consistently outperformed traditional logistic regression and simpler models, achieving the highest AUC and precision scores (Takawira, 2024).Meanwhile, Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Naïve Bayes delivered notably weaker performance, often ranking at the bottom of the ML methods tested (Takawira, 2024). In broader credit risk applications, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have occasionally surpassed boosting methods. Bhandary and Ghosh (2025) reported that DNNs outperformed Random Forest and XGBoost for Taiwanese credit card default prediction when measured by F1 score and AUC. However, kernel based methods like SVM, while outperforming linear models slightly, consistently ranked below ensemble and deep learning techniques in both sovereign and microcredit contexts (Bhandary & Ghosh, 2025; Arram et al., 2023). These findings emphasise that while modern ML algorithms offer large gains in accuracy, careful model selection is essential, some advanced methods like DNNs may only marginally outperform well tuned ensemble models, and simpler models like Support Vector Machine (SVM) may be inadequate for capturing complex, nonlinear interactions in sovereign default data.




Over the past two decades, the application of machine learning in sovereign debt and credit risk prediction has progressed significantly, driven by advances in computational power, richer datasets, and algorithmic sophistication. Early efforts in the 2000s primarily relied on static models like decision trees and support vector machines, but by the 2010s, gradient boosting ( XGBoost) emerged as a dominant approach due to its ability to handle high dimensional data and capture nonlinear patterns (Chen & Guestrin, 2016). However, LSTMs require long, dense datasets, while XGBoost handles sparsity better making a hybrid more realistic for sovereign debt contexts.Concurrently, the introduction of recurrent neural networks, especially long short term memory (LSTM) architectures, enabled researchers to model time dependent features and dependencies across multi year economic sequences (Siami‑Namin & Siami Namin, 2018). More recently, hybrid models combining ensemble methods and deep learning ( XGBoost + LSTM) demonstrated notable improvements in credit risk forecasting. For example, Abid (2025) showed that a hybrid XGBoost + LSTM model outperformed standalone methods in predicting sovereign credit risk in MENA countries, particularly during periods of political stress. Similarly, Consoli et al. (2022) used LSTM networks to forecast Italian sovereign bond spreads more effectively than traditional econometric benchmarks. These methodological innovations have led to steady gains in predictive accuracy and the ability to handle temporal dynamics. Thus the challenge of interpretability persisted, spurring recent efforts to integrate SHAP with hybrid models for transparency in sovereign debt forecasting. This trajectory highlights the iterative refinement of tools, from simple models to powerful hybrid architectures, and now toward models that are not only accurate but also explainable providing the foundation for our proposed hybrid XGBoost + attention LSTM framework enhanced with SHAP. Machine learning models frequently achieve higher AUROC but often lack calibration checks, making outputs difficult to use for policy thresholds. Many studies underplay the risk of overfitting given the small number of default events globally, which limits external validity.
213	2.5.1 Variable Selection and Justification in Sovereign Risk Models
214	For a robust understanding of sovereign default risk, macroeconomic , political , fiscal and external variables are required to know which of these factors will influence a country's national debt sustainability . Debt to GDP ratio is among the most commonly known and used indicators , acting as a proxy for a country's indebtedness in relation to its economic capacity (Manase et al, 2003) . When extended into broader definitions that include liabilities or short term external debt , it offers a deeper insight into solvency conditions. Real GDP growth and its annual changes capture the economy’s resilience and ability to generate income for debt servicing. Several studies have shown that declining growth often foresees sovereign stress events , particularly when combined with rigid fiscal structures (Furtes, Kalotychou, 2007). Real GDP capital reflects underlying economic development and often correlates with institutional strength , another critical component of default vulnerability .

Inflation , fiscal balance , and trade openness are also some of the other key factors in determining a sovereign default risk . High inflation often signals monetary instability , which can deter investment and erode debt holders’ confidence , especially in developing economies (Savon &Vezzoli, 2015). A persistent fiscal deficit undermines credibility and can signal unsustainable borrowing , while trade openness influences external vulnerability to global shock . The inclusion of external factors like the US Federal Funds Rate and the global oil price index account for systematic spillovers and international market conditions, both of which have been shown to significantly affect borrowing cost and liquidity access in not only emerging markets but developed ones as well (Reinhart, Rogoff,2009).political variables such as election years , help capture the effect of electoral cycles on fiscal behavior, as governments may engage in opportunistic spending or delay reforms phenomena linked to heightened default probability in multiple empirical models (Mallucci , 2021), by integrating these diverse but theoretically grounded indicators into these. It aims to create a data driven model of sovereign risk detection that highlights both internal fundamentals and external pressure .
215	Aside from the traditional macroeconomic indicators , several alternative factors have been proposed to enhance the accuracy of sovereign default prediction models. Credit ratings from international agencies, though sometimes lagging, provide aggregated risk assessments that encapsulate market sentiment and institutional credibility (Mendoza & Ostry, 2008). Institutional quality indicators such as corruption indices, rule of law, and political stability have also shown predictive relevance, particularly in distinguishing between countries with similar economic profiles but differing governance standards (Kose et al., 2011). Exchange rate volatility and capital flow reversals are often early signs of external vulnerability, especially in economies with high levels of foreign denominated debt (Baldacci et al., 2011). Financial market indicators like sovereign bond spreads and CDS premiums are increasingly used as real time stress indicators, reflecting investor perception of default risk (Duan et al., 2012). Election years represent periods of heightened political uncertainty, which can directly affect sovereign creditworthiness. Theoretical and empirical studies like, Drazen & Easterly, 2001; Arellano, 2008 suggest that governments may pursue expansionary fiscal policies prior to elections, increasing debt burdens or reducing fiscal discipline, thereby elevating sovereign default risk. Moreover, shifts in political leadership during election years can alter debt management strategies and investor confidence. For these reasons, election year is included in this study as a binary explanatory variable to capture temporal spikes in sovereign risk associated with political cycles. Lastly, some studies claim climate risk metrics, such as exposure to extreme weather or long term environmental fragility, are emerging as critical variables in debt sustainability discussions, but  are uncertain due unpredictability  and to low lying countries with resource dependent nations  (Buhr et al., 2018). Incorporating such variables can provide a more holistic and forward looking view of sovereign risk, especially when combined with machine learning frameworks capable of handling multidimensional input.
217	2.6 Explainable AI in Sovereign Debt Prediction
218	Interpretability has gained traction with tools such as Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model Agnostic Explanations (LIME). For instance, Hacibedel and Qu (2022) illustrate SHAP’s efficacy in making ML models transparent by clearly attributing high predicted default risk to variables like high external debt and dwindling foreign reserves. A notable real world example comes from the peer‑to‑peer lending sector, where Ariza‑Garzón et al. (2020) showed that applying SHAP to an XGBoost credit‑risk model led to greater acceptance among regulators. Specifically, feature‑level insights revealed that borrowers with short credit histories were being unfairly flagged by the model, prompting a reevaluation of feature inclusion and model thresholds. This transparency not only reduced bias but also improved stakeholder trust and compliance. Attention layers do highlight input importance within sequences, but their outputs do not always correspond to true causal drivers in economic systems. Interpretability remains approximate, and over reliance on attention risks overstating the transparency of deep learning approaches.
220	Despite these advances, SHAP also has important limitations that complicate its use in high stakes settings. Firstly, computational cost is significant exact Shapley value calculation scales combinatorially, making SHAP slow or infeasible for large feature sets without approximations (Data Overload, 2022). Second, attribution results can be misleading or unstable when features are correlated; SHAP’s method marginalises over feature coalitions and may overstate or understate a feature’s importance (Stats.StackExchange user, 2021). Third, approximation errors are common datasets evaluated with TreeSHAP sometimes produce attribution rankings that diverge significantly from exact Shapley values, potentially leading to misinterpretation (Van den Broeck et al., 2020; Letoffe et al., 2024). Finally, the technical complexity of SHAP outputs can overwhelm non expert stakeholders, reducing practical transparency in policy contexts (Yang et al., 2023). These challenges mean that while SHAP improves explainability relative to black box ML models, its own opacity and computational demands can inadvertently obscure rather than clarify model insights.
221	In the context of sovereign debt prediction, these limitations highlight the need for a balanced approach. Rather than relying solely on SHAP outputs, models should integrate simpler summary metrics, feature level stability checks, and domain validation to ensure explanations are reliable and practically meaningful. This thesis addresses these challenges by combining SHAP with manual validation of feature effects, assessing correlation sensitivity, and simplifying explanations for policy audiences, ensuring that interpretability gains are not overshadowed by computational or conceptual complexity.
227	2.7 Hybrid Models and Ensemble Approaches
228	Hybrid modelling frameworks that combine econometric and machine learning techniques have gained traction for improving predictive power across various financial domains. For example, Li, Stasinakis, and Yeo (2022) developed a classifier that first uses XGBoost for feature selection and then employs a multilayer perceptron, resulting in a hybrid model that outperformed single method counterparts for credit risk assessment. Similarly, Chacko, Aravindhar, and Arokiasamy (2024) introduced a hybrid model integrating an RNN with XGBoost for credit scoring, which demonstrated a five to seven percentage point increase in classification accuracy compared to either model alone. These hybrid two stage frameworks have been applied successfully in credit and commodity markets, such as the ES‑RNN model in forecasting COVID‑19 mortality time series, which achieved notable reductions in forecast error metrics (Mathonsi & van Zyl, 2021).
229	While these hybrid structures enhance predictive accuracy by capturing both temporal and nonlinear feature interactions, their application to sovereign debt prediction remains limited. To the best of known knowledge, no prior study in sovereign default forecasting combines an attention‑based LSTM with XGBoost and explainable AI in a single hybrid architecture. This thesis addresses that gap by explicitly integrating sequential modelling of macro political indicators, nonlinear boosting for classification, and SHAP based interpretability, creating a transparent and empirically robust framework for real world sovereign risk analysis.Hybrid models combine strengths of ML and econometrics, but their complexity poses challenges for real world adoption. Literature rarely considers the trade off between marginal performance gains and usability for policymakers who may lack technical expertise.XGBoost is powerful for capturing nonlinearity and interactions, yet it is prone to overfitting in smaller or noisy sovereign debt datasets. Careful validation is required since default parameter settings may exaggerate performance when applied without rigorous tuning.
234	2.8 Existing Literature
235	While numerous hybrid and explainable ML approaches have emerged, few have successfully bridged the gap between technical performance and policy adoption in sovereign debt contexts. In related domains such as credit risk and fraud detection, interpretability has demonstrated practical influence. For instance, Jung et al. (2019) and Hadji Misheva et al. (2021) demonstrate that SHAP powered explanations in credit scoring not only improved regulatory acceptance but also helped reveal and address feature level biases in lending decisions, thereby enhancing transparency and stakeholder trust. Similarly, Mathonsi and van Zyl (2021) and Chacko et al. (2024) report that applying a hybrid LSTM + XGBoost model to financial time series not only improved forecast accuracy but also leveraged explainability to inform corporate risk management choices. These examples imply that explainable AI tools can play a pivotal role in making complex models actionable and credible in high stakes financial decision making. Moreover, applying this concept to sovereign default prediction warrants caution. Unlike credit scoring or fraud detection, where event rates are relatively higher and patterns more structured, sovereign defaults are rare events with significant heterogeneity across countries and time. The inclusion of such rare outcomes may impede model effectiveness by intensifying class imbalance, limiting learning opportunities for complex classifiers, and possibly reducing the reliability of post hoc explainability methods like SHAP, which depend on sufficient variation in both features and outcomes to generate stable and meaningful explanations. Thus, while explainable AI may enhance transparency, its integration into sovereign default models must carefully account for the challenges posed by sparsity, imbalance, and noise inherent in this domain. This highlights a tension, the very rarity of sovereign defaults. The reason early warnings are valuable  may also constrain the effectiveness and interpretability of even the most sophisticated machine learning frameworks.
236	However, in the realm of sovereign debt prediction, explicit hybrid models combining sequence aware learning, nonlinear classification, and explainable AI are scarce. Previous work in sovereign risk has largely focused on standalone econometric or tree based models (Alaminos et al., 2021; Petropoulos et al., 2022) or used SHAP for post hoc interpretation (Hacibedel & Qu, 2022), but not within a structured hybrid architecture. This thesis directly addresses these gaps by integrating attention based LSTM and XGBoost within a single framework, enhanced with SHAP based explanations. The novelty lies not just in layering models, but in delivering a transparent, policy ready tool that policymakers and financial institutions can interpret, trust, and act upon rather than just observe through a black box lens.Some may argue that well tuned logistic or probit models are sufficient for sovereign default prediction, particularly due to their transparency and ease of estimation. Thus, this position underestimates the limitations these models face in capturing nonlinear interactions and temporal dynamics, especially in rare event contexts. As demonstrated in Section 4, logistic and probit models in this study produced near random classification performance, underscoring their limitations. Furthermore, interpretability alone is insufficient if the model lacks sufficient discriminatory power to inform policy decisions.

241	2.8.2 Predictive Performance vs. Simpler Models
242	Across domains, evidence is mixed on whether complex ML models consistently outperform traditional simpler approaches. On one hand, several studies in economic and financial forecasting report gains from ML. For example, Bluwstein et al. (2023) apply ensemble tree methods to predict financial crises and find these flexible ML models significantly outperform a standard logistic regression early warning model. By calibrating models to catch 80% of crises, they show the best ML model can cut false alarms almost in half relative to logistic regression, from 18% to 31% false positives. This highlights ML’s strength in capturing complex nonlinear interactions among macro financial indicators, joint effects of credit booms and yield curve changes, that simpler models might miss. A clear strength of such research is demonstrating improved predictive power and new insights ,they identify rapid credit growth and yield curve inversions as key warning signals. Likewise, in sovereign risk forecasting, Hellwig (2021) finds that traditional econometric crisis models failed to beat even a naïve “rule of thumb,” whereas ML techniques such as random forests, gradient boosting which delivered significant out of sample accuracy improvements, especially after expanding the set of input predictors beyond the usual few variables. This suggests that when richer data are available, ML can harness it more effectively than linear models, a notable contribution since it underlines the importance of data breadth and algorithmic feature selection for improving forecasts.
243	On the other hand, multiple studies caution that ML’s edge is not universal. In data scarce settings, incremental gains can be modest. Badia et al. (2022) argue that with limited crisis observations, highly complex models risk overfitting noise and may fail to outperform parsimonious early warning models. Similarly, Chu & Qureshi (2023) compare popular methods for U.S. GDP growth forecasting and find that while ensemble ML bagging, boosting yields slight improvements at short horizons, for longer term forecasts, the differences vanish; a simple model using one strong predictor can outperform far more complex ML models. Such findings emphasise that context matters. ML can excel with high dimensional data and short term patterns, but for small samples or long horizon/macroeconomic predictions, well specified simpler models remain competitive. The quality of these comparative studies is generally high, Chu & Qureshi’s analysis is peer reviewed and rigorously out of sample, lending credibility to the result that complexity has diminishing returns in certain regimes. Overall, the literature suggests that researchers must justify added complexity with clear evidence of predictive gains; otherwise, simpler approaches with their robustness and transparency may suffice. While Chu & Qureshi (2023) advocate for the use of single predictor models in long horizon GDP forecasting, their findings do not necessarily generalize to sovereign default contexts. Unlike GDP, which is continuous and often smoother than national debt defaults , defaults are binary, infrequent, and subject to structural breaks. Thus, relying on one or two predictors may miss key turning points a risk policymakers cannot afford.Our study addresses this by carefully choosing model complexity appropriate for the data at hand and by statistically testing whether more complex techniques truly add predictive value over simpler benchmarks, rather than assuming ML is always superior.
245	These mixed findings emphasize that predictive accuracy alone is insufficient: methodological appropriateness and interpretability must be contextualized to dataset characteristics and user needs. Our study explicitly acknowledges this by tailoring model complexity to the sovereign default domain, where rare events and heterogeneous economic structures make transparency as vital as accuracy.While debt sustainability models provide useful thresholds, it may assume rational governments and efficient markets, which rarely hold in practice. Political incentives, hidden liabilities, and data opacity mean defaults often occur outside these predicted thresholds, exposing the limited predictive power of classical theory.
247	2.8.3 Opacity and Interpretability
248	A recurring critique in the literature especially from policymakers and regulators is the opacity of complex ML models. Even when ML improves predictions, lack of interpretability can impede trust and practical adoption. For instance, Bluwstein et al. They acknowledge this as an “important issue” for policy use and respond by extracting Shapley values to rank predictors, an approach that reveals intuitive results, private credit growth, and yield curve slope as top contributors. This illustrates a strength of recent research. not only pushing accuracy, but also exploring explainable AI (XAI) tools to interpret models.
249	In the credit risk domain, where decisions affect borrowers and fall under strict regulation, scholars echo similar concerns. Chen et al. (2023) observe that banks have hesitated to deploy more complex classifiers despite potential accuracy gains, noting that although complex ML models may improve accuracy over simple logistic regressions, their limited interpretability has inhibited their adoption in credit risk assessment. Indeed, logistic regression remains the industry standard for credit scoring due to its transparency. Chen et al.’s contribution is a two stage “selective” model that tries to get the best of both worlds. It uses a simple logistic model for the majority of cases and falls back on a shallow neural network for instances where the linear model is insufficient, thus boosting accuracy on difficult cases while maintaining interpretability overall. They find most credit accounts are adequately handled by a logistic model, a reassuring point about the strength of simpler models, and only a minority require the nonlinear model, an elegant demonstration of balancing complexity with transparency in practice.
250	SHAP values are often adopted in financial risk modelling because they decompose predictions into feature contributions, offering a level of transparency absent in most black-box models. However, their stability is sensitive to feature correlation, a common property in macroeconomic datasets where debt, inflation, and growth are intertwined. In such contexts, SHAP may inflate the importance of collinear variables or mask causal direction, potentially misleading policymakers who expect interpretable signals for intervention. While SHAP remains more theoretically grounded than LIME, its reliance on additive explanations means it can oversimplify nonlinear dynamics, underlining the need for cautious integration rather than unquestioned acceptance.
251	Although SHAP values offer valuable local explanations, they carry important limitations in correlated settings. Kumar et al. (2021) demonstrate that, in nonlinear and correlated contexts, SHAP values may fail to capture true feature effects highlighted by the introduction of Shapley Residuals as diagnostic tools. Similarly, simulation results show that even modest correlations around 0.05 can distort SHAP values significantly, sometimes reversing their signs. In sovereign debt datasets where multicollinearity among, say, GDP growth, inflation, and debt levels is commonplace these issues could mislead interpretation, suggesting a need for cautious use.

By doing so, the aim is to make the model’s decisions more transparent to policymakers, helping bridge the gap between predictive performance and practical explainability. This approach directly covers a key shortcoming identified in prior research, this demonstrates that it’s possible to have a model that is both accurate and interpretable, thereby enhancing its policy relevance.

277	3.2.2 Cross‑Validation and Robustness Checks
278	To validate the stability and generalizability of our findings, nested cross validation will be applied during model tuning and comparative evaluation, a method recommended for time dependent financial datasets (NumAnalytic, 2022; CambridgeSpark, 2018) . In the outer loop, the data is split into training and testing folds, preserving temporal order to prevent data leakage. Within each outer fold, an inner loop performs hyperparameter selection via k-fold cross validation and early stopping. This ensures that performance estimates are unbiased by model selection.

This simulates a real world policy monitoring environment and confirms that model performance remains consistent across changing economic regimes.

566	4.5 Robustness Checks
567	To assess the robustness and generalizability of the predictive models, stratified five fold cross validation was employed. This approach ensures that each fold maintains the original class distribution, which is particularly important given the significant class imbalance in the dataset, where defaults account for only approximately 1.4 per cent of observations. The dataset was partitioned into five subsets; in each iteration, four subsets were used for training and one for validation, cycling through all subsets to ensure that every observation was used for both training and validation exactly once. This procedure mitigates the risk of performance results being driven by a single random split, providing a more reliable estimate of model accuracy and F1-score. The choice of 5 folds balances computational efficiency and statistical rigour, offering sufficient variability across splits while avoiding the excessive computational burden that would result from using a higher number of folds, such as 10. This cross validation strategy is consistent with best practices in empirical machine learning studies involving medium sized, imbalanced datasets and ensures that model evaluation remains fair, robust, and replicable.The following section interprets these results, comparing them with existing literature and evaluating their implications for sovereign default risk prediction

655	5.4 Limitations of the Study
656	Despite these contributions, this research is not without limitations. A major limitation concerns data quality and availability. Many low income countries had incomplete reporting histories, leading to their exclusion and introducing potential selection bias. Although SMOTE and stratified cross validation were used to mitigate class imbalance , sovereign defaults accounted for just 1.4% of observations. Rare event prediction remains inherently difficult, as reflected in moderate recall rates for defaults. Furthermore, while SHAP improves transparency, some internal feature representations of the LSTM remain obscure and difficult to interpret intuitively.
657	Despite the use of SMOTE to address severe class imbalance, the hybrid model’s recall for defaults remained moderate, highlighting an intrinsic limitation when forecasting rare events such as sovereign defaults. SMOTE synthetically augments the minority class, but it cannot fully resolve the structural sparsity and heterogeneity of actual defaults in the data. The rarity and idiosyncrasy of sovereign defaults, influenced by unpredictable shocks and regime changes, pose a fundamental challenge that even sophisticated oversampling techniques cannot fully overcome. This limitation suggests that while machine learning improves discrimination relative to traditional models, inherent data sparsity places an upper bound on achievable recall in sovereign risk prediction.
658	Another limitation lies in the heterogeneity of sovereign risk environments. Sovereign defaults differ markedly across regions and institutional contexts, influenced by varying legal frameworks, exchange rate regimes, and political conditions. The global pooled model estimated here assumes uniform relationships across countries, which may obscure important regional dynamics. In addition, temporal non stationarity is a challenge, macroeconomic environments have evolved substantially over four decades, with events such as the Cold War’s end, the global financial crisis, and COVID-19 likely requiring distinct modelling approaches that this global framework cannot fully accommodate.
660	5.5 Risks of Model Misuse or Overreliance
661	Though the proposed hybrid model has strong forecasting ability and interpretability, it does present some risk or limitation that could make it fail or mislead under real world applications. A main concern is that it would become overly fitted to previous patterns that may not generalize under future crises, given that sovereign default is a rare occurrence that is heterogeneous. Macroeconomic shocks, geopolitical disruptions, or unprecedented policy shifts such as pandemics or global financial restructurings could render learned patterns outdated, leading to false signals or missed warnings. Overreliance on model outputs could also provide a false sense of security that makes policymakers overlook qualitative assessments, expert judgment, or early indicators that exceed data scope. Applied mechanistically without any continued validation based on interpretation of context, model predictions might entrench biases or introduce undue delay in timely interventions. In effect, while the model offers helpful support, it must be integrated with caution under a larger analytical and governing structure that includes human oversight along with adaptive learning.

665	5.7 SHAP Model Trade-offs and Anomalies
666	While SHAP enhances the interpretability of complex models, several limitations and trade offs deserve consideration. First, SHAP values assume feature independence and additive contributions, which may not hold in the presence of multicollinearity or strong feature interactions, a common scenario in macroeconomic datasets. This can lead to misleading attributions, where correlated features such as debt and deficit receive overlapping or unstable importance scores. Second, SHAP explanations can become computationally expensive and noisy when applied to deep architectures or high dimensional inputs, particularly with LSTM components where hidden states and temporal dependencies are not easily decomposed. Third, although SHAP ranks features by their average marginal contribution, it does not account for causal influence a high SHAP value does not imply that altering that feature would change the outcome. This may mislead policymakers into assuming controllability where none exists. For instance, if an election year receives a high SHAP value, it does not mean delaying elections would reduce default risk, it simply means election timing correlates with risk in historical patterns. Additionally, SHAP values vary depending on the background dataset used for comparison, if this reference set is skewed or unrepresentative, the resulting explanations may reflect artefacts rather than genuine relationships. Finally, while SHAP improves transparency over black box predictions, it can also introduce a false sense of confidence that complex models remain vulnerable to data drift, adversarial patterns, or regime shifts that SHAP cannot detect or explain. These trade offs suggest that SHAP outputs, while valuable, must be interpreted within a cautious, context aware, and expert guided framework to avoid overreliance or misapplication in sovereign risk policymaking.

682	In conclusion, this thesis makes a strong methodological and empirical contribution to sovereign default prediction research. By integrating machine learning, deep learning, and explainability, it delivers a replicable, scalable, and policy relevant early warning tool. The hybrid model not only improves predictive accuracy but also addresses a key concern of policymakers understanding the drivers of sovereign default risk, thereby narrowing the gap between advanced machine learning techniques and their adoption in critical, high stakes policy environments.
